\section*{Preamble}

Beyond the purely institutional and authoritarian traditions, the question of how far the biblical writings can be trusted has drawn the whole of Christendom into a trilemmatic labyrinth. Three currents dominate present-day thought -- the doctrine of absolute inerrancy, liberal theology, and anti-intellectualism -- and each, examined with epistemological care, proves a fundamental error.

On the one side stands the doctrine of absolute inerrancy, as it was classically set forth in the Chicago Statement \citep{icbi1978}. The axiom of a flawless, contradiction-free text has its historical roots in the North American Protestantism of the nineteenth century \citep{rogersmckim1979}, and it is today pressed to the point of absurdity -- for an absolute claim upon the literary form of the text can be derived neither from Scripture itself nor from logic. Where it is nonetheless upheld, it compels conclusions that no one of sound common sense would accept: that bats are birds (Lev 11:19), that insects walk on four legs (Lev 11:20--23), or that the earth is canopied by a solid vault with waters above it (Gen 1:6--8) \citep{wells2013}. And it has rent Christendom apart in the fundamentalist controversy \citep{marsden2006}. At the same time it binds faith to the flawless perfection of the text in every last detail, so that with the first admitted contradiction the whole of faith collapses for many \citep{ehrman2009}. The classical historical-grammatical method occupies a special place here: it claims to read the text with the tools of philology and historical scholarship, and yet refuses to acknowledge what follows from its own axiom. An honest analysis of grammar and context cannot but encounter incompatible worldviews, logical fallacies, and contradictions -- findings before which the postulate of absolute inerrancy simply cannot stand. Whoever clings to that premise must, in the end, discard the results of his own method and lapse into the strained harmonization that he mistakes for historical integrity. On the other side stands liberal theology, with its methodological atheism. It assumes the unfounded dogma of a closed causal nexus \citep{troeltsch1913,schlatter1905}, dismisses every account of divine action from the outset as myth \citep{bultmann1941}, and so strips revelation of its historical core. And finally, anti-intellectualism falls victim to the fallacy that no interpretation is needed at all -- ``it is right there, plain as day'' -- thereby raising its own unexamined subjectivity to the measure of truth \citep{noll1994}.

\section*{I. Why the established paths fail}

These three erroneous paths rest on a single hermeneutical category error: they miss the true speech act and intention of the text, because they misunderstand, conflate, or devalue its ancient literary form. Each in its own way overlooks what the ancient witness sought to do and to achieve by writing:

\subpar{(1) \textbf{Fundamentalism} absolutizes the outward form of the text. It assumes that a divinely inspired text must meet a standard of errorless truth in every linguistic and ideological detail; and when incompatible worldviews, logical errors, or contradictions surface, the result is either a strained harmonization or a faith exposed to cognitive blackmail \citep{barr1977}.}

\subpar{(2) \textbf{Liberal theology} answers this claim to absoluteness with a mirror-image confusion. Because it cannot -- or will not -- distinguish the ancient form of the text, with its worldviews and conventions, from the reality attested within it, it dismisses the whole as myth, captive to a closed causal nexus. It sacrifices the real core of the events because it cannot take the ancient literary form seriously without taking it literally \citep{schlatter1905,machen1923}.}

\subpar{(3) \textbf{Anti-intellectualism}, finally, severs the historical reference altogether. By claiming that Scripture explains itself without any mediation, it makes its own unexamined, present-day experience the measure of truth, and substitutes a subjective sense of edification for the historical intention of the author \citep{noll1994}.}

All three currents stumble over the same fault: they reduce the text to what it outwardly presents, rather than asking what it points to.

Beyond these three, other influential approaches remain -- Karl Barth's dialectical theology, Bultmann's existentialist interpretation, the modern canonical-narrative hermeneutics \citep{barth1922,bultmann1941,childs1979,frei1974} -- all of which take the real core of the history to be, in the first place, an existential address, a myth, or a purely narrative world. Though they mean to avoid the dead ends of inerrancy, they lapse, unlike the three pragmatic errors above, into a fundamental epistemological circularity: they affirm the truth of Scripture out of its own internal system, whether by a sovereign Spirit-event in the hearer, by the self-evidence of the act of faith, or by the self-contained narrative logic of the canon. In severing the meaningfulness of the testimony from its historical verifiability, they presuppose from the outset exactly what remains to be shown. Such circularity is epistemologically worthless, for it builds a closed system that immunizes itself, acquires no genuine knowledge, and refuses any rational accountability \citep{popper1963}. It was precisely this circularity that Pannenberg \citeyearpar{pannenberg1961,pannenberg1973} exposed with respect to the self-evidence of revelation, and Barr \citeyearpar{barr1983} and Tracy \citeyearpar{tracy1981} with respect to the canonical narrative logic.

If neither the strained smoothing of the literary form, nor the materialistic dissection, nor the naive refusal of careful textual work points toward a viable path, what remains?

A way out of this fundamental failure of the established paths is offered by the approach developed here, which we shall call the \textit{Approbatio Testimonii Historica} (\apth).

\section*{II. Foundations of the \textit{Approbatio Testimonii Historica}}

\apthsec{1}{Methodological Core Idea}{kerngedanke} The \apth joins the epistemological foundation of critical realism, a clear axiomatic derivation, and the established instruments of historical scholarship. Within its framework we meet the biblical testimony on equal footing with scientific reason, and with complete integrity.

\apthsec{2}{Distinction between Fact and Format}{faktum-format} 
The \apth draws a strict line between the ontological facticity of God's action in space and time and the literary form of the ancient tradition. This is not a special hermeneutical rule but a fundamental semiotic fact: no text in human history is identical with the reality to which it refers, for language is always its symbolic representation. Language is never the event itself but -- to speak in metaphor -- the map of a landscape. Whoever confuses form and fact tries to walk upon the map; whoever throws the map away because it is only paper loses all sense of direction. In this, the \apth takes up the classical \textit{doctrine of the two books}: God reveals himself in the ``book of nature,'' the empirical reality open to reason and science, and in the ``book of Scripture,'' the symbolic attestation of faith. Form and fact thus stand not in contradiction but in a complementary relation of reference.

\apthsec{3}{Axiom I: The Existence of God}{axiom-i} 
\textit{The world is the ordered product of a transcendent, personal cause.}
Space, time, and matter are created reality. Their order and their very describability point to a transcendent, personal cause -- to God, the personal source of space, time, and matter.

\apthsec{4}{Axiom II: Historical Traces of Transcendence}{axiom-ii} 
\textit{Divine action in reality leaves historically graspable effects.}
A personal creator God does not stand aloof from his creation. His action in space and time leaves empirically observable traces and effects in human history, and these lie open to historical analysis.

\apthsec{5}{Epistemological Function of Axioms I \& II}{axiom-funktion} 
\textit{Axioms define the scientific space of possibility without circularity.}
They involve no circularity (cf. Chapter I), for they prescribe no interpretive result. Rather, they make divine action thinkable in the first place, within the space of historical hypotheses.

\apthsec{6}{Axiom III: The Biblical Canon as a Specific Sphere of Efficacy}{kanon} 
\textit{The historical sphere of transcendent traces is located axiomatically in the biblical canon.}
As a third axiom we affirm that the historical traces of divine action (Axiom II) find their specific, historically attested frame of reference in the corpus of the Old and New Testaments. To delimit the object of research with precision, and to spare exegesis an infinite regress, we define the whole of this field of inquiry by the closed Protestant canon of 66 books: the Old Testament -- in technical terms the Hebrew canon (Tanakh), with its three parts Torah (Instruction), Nevi'im (Prophets), and Ketuvim (Writings), in the Protestant count of 39 books -- together with the New Testament, the collection of 27 writings fixed in the early church \citep{beckwith1985,metzger1987}.

\apthsec{7}{Heuristic Plausibility of the Canonical Boundaries}{kanon-heuristik} 
The axiomatic choice of the canon reflects the concentration of the primary testimonies as history has actually transmitted them. The delimitation of the field of inquiry (§~\ref{sec:kanon}) is a methodological heuristic: it rests on the historical fact that the ancient communities recognized, in precisely these 66 writings, a concentrated tradition of transcendent effects. The selection made by the early church -- its appeal to eyewitnesses, its concern for coherence, its broad reception -- serves the \apth not as an infallible dogma but as a historical indication that this corpus constitutes the primary source for divine action in reality. The choice of this particular canon is a pragmatic stipulation, not a dogmatic boundary. It must expressly not be mistaken for Reformed Christianity, or for any other confessional creed: the field of inquiry could in principle have been drawn otherwise. But every scholarly enterprise needs a boundary somewhere if it is to possess a clearly delineated object at all. Two considerations speak for the boundary of the 66 books of the Protestant canon. First, it marks the historically most conservative common ground shared by the greater part of Christendom; the further deuterocanonical writings of the Catholic or Orthodox canon widen the field without making it historically necessary. Second, this canon may be regarded, from the standpoint of its reception history, as a minimum: in precisely these texts the ancient church recognized and preserved the concentrated tradition of divine action.

\apthsec{8}{Extra-canonical Literature}{ausserkanonisch} From this follows our treatment of all remaining literature -- the Apocrypha, the Pseudepigrapha, the Qumran writings, the rabbinic tradition, and the literature of the Greco-Roman world. All of this is of the greatest value as historical context and as material for comparison, and the \apth draws upon it as such; but it lies outside the axiomatic field of inquiry. For these writings the axioms of §§~\ref{sec:axiom-i}--\ref{sec:kanon} do not apply as a claim about a sphere of divine action; they form the context of the canon, not its object of investigation. Their claim to truth is thereby neither affirmed nor contested. The same holds for later claims to revelation, such as the Qur'an: it might be investigated with the same instruments. Yet, because the author presupposes faith (the \textit{fiducia}), it carries no weight for the \apth as a source of transcendent traces. What is decisive is the abductive comparison: wherever different transcendent explanations must be weighed against one another, the researcher falls into no ideological conflict, precisely because the field of inquiry of the \apth expressly embraces only the canon. Within that field, the \apth then offers its monotheistically augmented historical methodology -- not a special dispensation, but the consistent application of the general instruments.

\apthsec{9}{Axiom IV: The Nature of the Text and Human Authorship}{textnatur}
\textit{The biblical writings exist as ancient source texts, composed by real, historical human beings.}
The canon (§~\ref{sec:kanon}) presents itself to the \apth as a collection of ancient source texts, composed by real, historical human beings, each in his own language, genre, and situation. In this the \apth parts company, by definition, with every doctrine of inspiration in the sense of dictation: the Bible is not the Qur'an. It is neither a magic formula dictated from heaven nor a word mechanically received. Stated soberly: what we know at the outset is that these texts bear witness to very different things -- events, interpretations, prayers, commandments, testimonies -- even where their genre is no historical narrative. What we do not know at the outset is that these writings arose per se from a wisdom granted by the Holy Spirit; such an assumption could not be made without circularity. One may of course arrive at such hypotheses by later reflection, by inference to the best explanation, for instance; but they would then be the result of research, not its presupposition. It is the existence, in principle, of this human authorial intention that first makes the study of the texts possible at all.

\section*{III. Methodological Structure of the \textit{Approbatio Testimonii Historica}}

\apthsec{10}{Speech Act Theory and the Determination of Authorial Intention}{sprechakttheorie} The \apth takes its concept of illocution from the speech act theory of the philosophy of language. John L. Austin \citeyearpar{austin1962} distinguished the \textit{locutionary act} (saying something with sense and reference), the \textit{illocutionary act} (what one does in saying it: to assert, to testify, to instruct), and the \textit{perlocutionary act} (the effect produced); the theory was systematized by John R. Searle \citeyearpar{searle1969}, while the underlying analysis of speaker intention stems from H. Paul Grice \citeyearpar{grice1957}. For the \apth this means, more precisely: form and fact (§~\ref{sec:faktum-format}) are a distinction drawn within the locutionary act, between the shape and sense of the utterance and the reality to which it points. The illocution, by contrast, is an independent dimension of its own -- the question of what the author did, with his words, to his addressees; and the perlocution is set apart from the illocution as its history of effects. This founds no special theological terminology. The reconstruction of illocutionary intention is an established procedure of historical scholarship, introduced into the history of ideas by Quentin Skinner \citeyearpar{skinner1969}. The \apth adopts it as part of the general historical repertoire, and it rejects those excesses that would raise a philosophical concept into an exclusive exegetical method -- most famously Rudolf Bultmann's \citeyearpar{bultmann1941} existentialist interpretation, which elevated Heidegger's existential analytic into a privileged hermeneutical key, and the new hermeneutics of Ernst Fuchs \citeyearpar{fuchs1959} and Gerhard Ebeling \citeyearpar{ebeling1967}, which made an absolute of Heidegger's philosophy of language as a ``language event.'' On this basis, the following holds for determining authorial intention: the distinction between the ancient literary form and the intended speech act (the illocution) must not be reversed into a convenient escape. A time-bound worldview, even one mistaken by our lights, can itself be the very object of the intended speech act. The \apth commits itself to determining authorial intention with historical honesty -- even where the illocution contains assertions that contradict our present knowledge or worldview. Where the author does not merely use an ancient conception as the vehicle of his utterance, but means to teach it or to take it for granted, that is precisely the finding that stands.

\apthsec{11}{Inference to the Best Explanation}{inferenz} To assess the transcendent traces presupposed in Axiom II (§~\ref{sec:axiom-ii}) without lapsing into the arbitrariness of selective interpretation, the \apth needs a clear methodological criterion for judging testimonies that intend something transcendent. Here we apply the criterion of \textit{inference to the best explanation} \citep{harman1965,lipton2004,wright1992}: a transcendent intervention of God is entertained by the \apth as the historically best hypothesis when it explains a genuinely graspable historical effect that would otherwise remain puzzling, incoherent, or historically improbable. We ask, therefore, not only what the text asserts, but which real cause most coherently explains the observable effects of history -- the rise of the early community, the radical change of the witnesses, the very structure of the tradition. The admission of divine action thus becomes not a blind leap of faith but a rationally accountable conclusion, resting on historical explanatory power. In this the \apth expressly constructs no ``God of the gaps'': divine action is not summoned as a stopgap for the holes in our knowledge. What matters is not the mere failure of immanent explanations, but positive explanatory power -- a genuinely graspable, historical effect rendered intelligible by the transcendent cause that is assumed. Where no such positive finding exists, the \apth raises no transcendent hypothesis at all; often an honest ``we do not know'' -- a \textit{non liquet} -- is then the best explanation. The inference rests upon the data, not upon the incapacity of reason \citep{bonhoeffer1951,coulson1955,plantinga2011}.

\apthsec{12}{Full Application of the Methods}{methodenanwendung} We apply the established instruments of historical-critical research without reserve: textual criticism (the restoration of the original wording), literary criticism (the separation of sources and editorial layers), form criticism (the analysis of small genres and their ``Sitz im Leben''), tradition and redaction criticism (the growth of the tradition and its final shape at the hand of the redactor), together with historical semantics and the history-of-religions comparison \citep{steck1999,schnelle2008,soeding1998}. The same holds for the instruments that classical scholarship has devised for ancient texts -- source criticism and the establishment of the text, epigraphy, papyrology, numismatics, and historical contextualization. The biblical writings are ancient texts, and they are treated with exactly these tools \citep{schaps2011,bernheim1889}. The text is examined in its own ancient language, its literary genre, and its historical setting. One point must be stressed: no special instrument is needed here -- not Drewermann's depth-psychological exegesis \citeyearpar{drewermann1984}, which stands as a warning counterexample; not some invented ``spiritual exegesis'' of the kind found in Steiner's anthroposophical reading of Scripture \citeyearpar{steiner1910} or Weinreb's number mysticism \citeyearpar{weinreb1963}; and no apologetic rules of exception. The established historical methods are wholly sufficient to understand the text in its human origin and its ancient reality. We need neither to alter nor to weaken the tools of research in order to protect faith; we need only to free them of ideological prejudice. One thing, however, must be stated plainly: the only modification the \apth makes concerns not the methodological repertoire itself, but solely the space of hypotheses.

\apthsec{13}{Monotheistically Augmented Historical Methods}{adjungierung} We reject the claim that historical research conducted without the assumption of God is purely ``methodologically agnostic,'' and therefore ideologically neutral -- an attitude met today above all as clinical bracketing \citep{meier1991}, and one that goes back historically to Troeltsch's \citeyearpar{troeltsch1913} methodological postulate of the closed causal nexus. In a reality created and shaped by God, the methodological bracketing of divine action is, in function, nothing other than methodological atheism \citep{schlatter1905,pannenberg1961,maier1974,linnemann1999}. Let it be said plainly: the \apth does not claim to reform the established historical guild as such, although a fundamental epistemological revision would indeed be in order. Its general instruments remain untouched in immanent matters, and are recognized within their own competence. What the \apth undertakes is narrower and more modest. For its clearly delimited object -- the biblical canon (§~\ref{sec:kanon}), in which, according to Axiom II, transcendent traces are attested -- it removes a blind spot. It adds the monotheistic premise, openly, to the existing historical methodology, instead of letting it operate invisibly as a criterion of exclusion, as the supposedly neutral agnosticism does; a procedure that mirrors the objection raised against the parallel case in the philosophy of science, methodological naturalism \citep{plantinga2011}. We call this procedure \textit{monotheistically augmented historical methods}. A genuine agnosticism, open to experience, differs from atheism precisely in this: in the historical abduction (§~\ref{sec:inferenz}) it is willing to admit divine action as the best explanation wherever purely immanent models fail against the historical evidence \citep{keener2011,licona2010,wright2003}. Such openness to assenting to the truth as handed down corresponds to the hermeneutical program of Stuhlmacher \citep{stuhlmacher1979,stuhlmacher1989} and to the critical realism of a Wright \citep{wright1992}. Because we profess monotheistic faith in God without reserve, the \apth makes no claim to be an ideologically neutral method. It is, rather, a rationally accountable set of instruments that presupposes faith in God as the transcendent cause of reality, and on that basis enables historical analysis. The \apth sets the established tools of historical scholarship free from the atheistic prejudice, and preserves historical integrity in an honest dialogue with the witnesses.

\apthsec{14}{Working Hypothesis: The Messianic Logic of Fulfillment}{erfuellungslogik} 
\textit{The internal relation between the Hebrew canon and the New Testament testimony serves as a regulative research paradigm.}
The working hypothesis runs in two directions, and in both it is framed as a falsifiable historical statement about the texts themselves, not as a faith already held:

\subpar{(1) \textbf{Forward direction.} The Tanakh is open toward fulfillment. Its promises -- the covenant pledge, the royal promise, the new covenant, the new exodus -- give rise to a surplus of expectation that reaches beyond any fulfillment within time, and in the late-prophetic and early-Jewish messianic hope they point expressly to a coming Anointed One \citep{zimmerli1952,vonrad1960,mowinckel1956,collins2010}.}

\subpar{(2) \textbf{Backward direction.} The New Testament authors presuppose this framework, and claim for Jesus and the early community that Israel's history and promises reach their eschatological fulfillment in him ``according to the Scriptures'' \citep{dodd1952,goppelt1939,hays1989,huebner1990,wright1992}. The Tanakh is thus no mere background, but the generative system of reference for the New Testament illocutions. This presupposition does not assert in advance the proof of any single passage; it describes the verifiable self-placement of the two corpora. As a working hypothesis it remains open to testing, and may be modified or refuted by the findings that follow from it.}

\section*{IV. Scientific Methodology and Faith}

\apthsec{15}{In Defense of the Intellect}{kategorial} The scientific method and intellectual understanding belong to a creation that has already come about. God has given human beings reason and logic, and an intellectual grasp of the intention of the text is the primary end of biblical interpretation. We distance ourselves, then, decisively from every anti-intellectualist current that shuts out reason and thereby sinks into a covert skepticism -- a danger described by Noll \citeyearpar{noll1994} and Moreland \citeyearpar{moreland1997}, and named and rejected by John Paul II \citeyearpar{fidesetratio1998} as fideism. It is worth saying in advance: even a mature theology does not condemn reason; it assigns it a firm place. The classical distinction of knowledge, assent, and trust (\textit{notitia, assensus, fiducia}) begins with the rational grasp of the testimony \citep{calvin1559}. The full unfolding of personal faith as \textit{fiducia} is not our concern here. At the same time it must be said soberly: a skeptic is hard, if not impossible, to persuade by rationality. Skepticism is a deep hole, and utterly immune to argument \citep{wittgenstein1969}. Some anti-intellectualist current even takes argument itself as confirmation of its worldview -- contradiction strengthens the conviction rather than shaking it \citep{festinger1956,noll1994}. To escape this hermeneutical paralysis requires philosophical ways out and practical steps of liberation. Epistemologically, absolute skepticism betrays itself as a pragmatic self-contradiction: whoever claims that nothing can be known for certain raises that very claim to the rank of knowledge. And doubt founders on everyday life, on common sense, since every action compels trust in perception and logic. No rhetorical pressure liberates from immunized thinking. What liberates is, first, Socratic questioning, which lays bare one's own criteria of falsification; second, an emotional security that allows opinion to be detached from identity without loss of face; and third, the lived example of epistemic humility and hermeneutical magnanimity (§~\ref{sec:tugenden}), which deprives the system of its enemy image.

\apthsec{16}{Appreciation of the Charge of Scientism}{wissenschaftsglaeubigkeit} At this point an objection deserves a sincere hearing, one that comes above all from the anti-intellectualist side: whoever examines the Bible with the methods of science, it is said, makes modern science the cornerstone of truth. In all candor: a ``naked,'' presuppositionless reading of the Bible, free of every lens, exists only in the imagination of Sunday sermons. Every interpretation works with a prior understanding; presuppositionless exegesis is impossible \citep{bultmann1957,gadamer1960}. The only reading genuinely free of spectacles is the ``gut feeling'' -- that is, true is whatever produces a pleasant shiver while reading\textinterrobang\space Whoever does not wish to end up on the esoteric shelf must choose a pair of spectacles. Every interpretation works, unavoidably, with a prior understanding; the real question is therefore not whether one wears spectacles, but which. That the \apth chooses modern science has a simple reason: it is, by everything we know, the best we presently have. The choice of the historical-critical and analytical instruments springs not from a naive faith in the spirit of the age, but from methodological necessity: they form the most consistent, intersubjectively verifiable system yet devised for examining historical source texts as to their origin and intention. Whoever forgoes scientific tools inevitably exchanges intersubjective verifiability for unchecked subjectivity. Other spectacles have been criticized in this manifesto, each on its own grounds, and no longer stand open as alternatives.

\apthsec{17}{Exegesis and the Critique of the Present Age}{exegese-zeitkritik} The methodological reach of exegesis is clearly bounded. Exegesis (from the Greek ἐξήγησις, ``interpretation, exposition'') is the methodically controlled, historical-critical interpretation of a text; its end is the reconstruction of the purposeful speech act -- the illocution -- of the ancient author in his historical context \citep{stuhlmacher1989}. With the reconstruction of that illocution its task is complete. Everything beyond it concerns, according to \citet{hirsch1967}, no longer the meaning of the text (\textit{meaning}) but its significance (\textit{significance}) for later contexts. What follows thereafter is therefore no longer exegesis, but something else:

\subpar{(1) \textbf{Delimitation from reception history.} Reception history is an independent, legitimate object of research (the separation of perlocution from reception history is grounded in §~\ref{sec:sprechakttheorie}): \citet{gadamer1960} has shown that no understanding stands outside the history of a text's effects, and \citet{luz1985} has developed it as an exegetical perspective in its own right. It does not, however, answer the question of what the ancient author did with his text. For the reconstruction of that illocution, reception history is therefore consulted only insofar as it sharpens the view of the ancient text -- as a heuristic indicator, not as an object of judgment.}

\subpar{(2) \textbf{Delimitation from the critique of movements.} The evaluation of other movements or of present-day communities is a task of systematic theology and of the study of confessions, not an exegetical judgment. Whoever turns a passage of text into a weapon against others has himself abandoned the illocution of the text, and fallen into the very hidden error he fights elsewhere: he no longer reads what the ancient witness meant to do, but what his own present-day polemic requires.}

\apthsec{18}{The Demarcation of Exegesis}{abgrenzung-exegese}
We thus draw a strict line between what the text says and intends, and what later readers make of it \citep{hirsch1967}. Only so does exegesis remain historically honest, and preserved from the misuse of the text as a weapon of ideological dispute. Nor does the work end with exegesis: a developed exegesis serves as a point of reference from which reception-historical judgments or a concrete critique of movements may be undertaken. Theologically, this is the classical distinction between exegesis -- the historical reconstruction of the speech act -- and hermeneutics in the broader sense, which also embraces the application of the text to the present; the word ``hermeneutics'' carries many meanings \citep{ebeling1959}. The distinction goes back to the older Protestant hermeneutics, which distinguished, alongside understanding (\textit{subtilitas intelligendi}) and interpretation (\textit{subtilitas explicandi}), application (\textit{subtilitas applicandi}) in particular \citep{rambach1723}; \citet{gadamer1960} recovered this moment of application as an indispensable part of understanding itself. These further steps, however, are not taken up by the \apth.

\apthsec{19}{Refutation of the Charge of Dilution}{verwaesserung} From the conservative or fundamentalist corner an objection may be raised at this point: whoever uses these methods, it is said, inevitably slides into liberal theology and dissolves faith. The objection, however, rests on an error of reasoning, for it confuses the analytical tool with the atheistic axiom of liberal exegesis \citep{schlatter1905,troeltsch1913}. To use the historical method is not to dismantle faith \citep{stuhlmacher1979,wright1992,enns2005,sparks2008}. The slide into liberal theology does not come through the analysis of language or sources, but only when one adopts the postulate that God cannot intervene in history \citep{harvey1966,lessing1777}. Since our model of thought expressly excludes this atheistic postulate through the chain of axioms that precedes it, the substance of faith in revelation remains wholly untouched. Thorough textual research thus becomes not the enemy of faith, but the guarantor of a historical integrity that shields against naïveté no less than against secularism.

\apthsec{20}{Against Selective Relativization}{selektive-relativierung} Equally, this stance guards against the opposite extreme, an overly symbolic reinterpretation: the claim, for instance, that God exists and acts, but that the cross is no more than a timeless symbol of love, or the resurrection merely an image for the survival of an idea. The distinction from what precedes matters here: this relativization does not spring from the atheistic axiom, but is found among people who certainly believe in God -- who simply refuse to believe certain things. Such a selective clinging to what is agreeable, while what offends is symbolically defused, is not an honest reading but wishful thinking: it keeps the shell of faith and empties its content -- in substance, another religion \citep{machen1923}. The historical methods show unmistakably that the New Testament witnesses meant to convey no timeless symbols, but assert that real, incisive events took place in space and time \citep{wright2003}. Whoever is unwilling to believe any one of these testimonies must reject it honestly; to soften it into metaphor while believing the rest is neither historically nor existentially honest \citep{lewis1967,bultmann1941}. A kindred form of the same error seeks, in the miracles, the ``real core'' in a naturally explainable process -- Jesus walking on the lake as wading across a sandbank, or the feeding of the five thousand as shared provisions -- and so clings to the shell of the event while dissolving the miracle as miracle \citep{paulus1828}.

\apthsec{21}{No Deep Harmonization}{tiefen-harmonisierung} The \apth expressly does not demand that one infer, from the recognizable contradictions, errors, or inconsistencies of a text, some higher principle that nevertheless answers to absolute truth. Yet precisely this short-circuit is performed today by certain movements: where the text shows tensions or errors, they suppose a hidden depth that is flawless and absolutely true -- above all in the doctrine of the \textit{sensus plenior} \citep{brown1955} and in the apologetic harmonization of alleged contradictions \citep{archer1982}. Such an assumption is an imputation upon the human author: we cannot know, and we do not claim, that the ancient author aimed at any such principle with his words. Even where the text attests divine action, no obligation follows: divine truth may indeed stand behind a testimony, yet there is no duty to assume, in every case, a higher principle that resolves the contradictions. Historical-critical research has rather laid bare the diversity and the tensions of the canon as given, and has rejected artificial harmonization \citep{kaesemann1951,dunn1977,enns2005}. The \apth forces no deep harmonization; it takes the text as seriously as the text is.

\apthsec{22}{Against the Collapse Objection}{kollaps} Proponents of absolute inerrancy often answer at this point with a seemingly compelling argument: \textit{If we admit contradictions in the text, how can we still believe in the cross?} Whoever concedes a single error, so the objection runs, has surrendered the whole of Scripture; freedom from contradiction is the pillar on which faith rests. The objection rests on a twofold error of reasoning, and the \apth can name it precisely.

\subpar{(1) \textbf{The confusion of fact and format.} The objection treats Scripture as a homogeneous, propositional system, in which every statement carries the same system-critical weight. A conflict in ancient chronology or cosmology (form) is then supposed to shake the kerygmatic center (fact) at once. This is a category error. The raising of Christ from the dead is an event in space and time; the question of how many years the drought under Elijah lasted is a feature of the ancient shape of the tradition. The truth of the one does not hang upon the perfection of the other \citep{wright2005}.}

\subpar{(2) \textbf{The wrong root.} Faith in the cross and the resurrection does not rest on a deductive chain (``because every sentence of the Bible is true, therefore the cross is also true''), but on the inference to the best explanation (cf. §~\ref{sec:inferenz}). The resurrection explains the radical change in the conduct of the witnesses, the rise of the early community, and the readiness for martyrdom more coherently than any purely immanent alternative. This explanatory power is untouched by the Evangelists' divergent perspectives on detail, or by shifts of tradition in peripheral matters. On the contrary: small inconsistencies, differing perspectives, and editorial peculiarities by no means lessen credibility. In historical scholarship such human refractions count as strong evidence against collusion or later forgery; it is precisely the errors and nuances of the human witnesses that render their accounts the more historically credible. Whoever stakes everything on freedom from contradiction as the foundation has built faith upon the most fragile of all pillars \citep{bauckham2006}.}

\subpar{(3) \textbf{The incarnation analogy and the historical order.} The objection also overlooks that Scripture, by analogy with the incarnation, bears wholly human features: as Christ is fully human and fully God, so Scripture is a wholly human word, bound to a culture and an age -- and precisely thereby a testimony to divine action \citep{enns2005}. The historical order confirms this: the early community believed in the cross and the resurrection long before a single New Testament text existed. Faith in the event came before the text, not the reverse. Whoever makes faith depend upon an error-free text turns this order upside down \citep{sparks2008}.}

\section*{V. Application}

\apthsec{23}{The Necessity of Intellectual Virtues}{tugenden} Since every historical and hermeneutical method meets human beings who must decide under conditions of incompleteness and imprecision, the \apth requires, alongside the scientific instruments, the following intellectual virtues \citep{zagzebski1996}:

\subpar{(1) \textbf{Intellectual honesty (\textit{Honestas Epistemica}):} The incorruptible readiness to let a finding stand as it presents itself, even when it damages or calls into question one's own preferences, apologetic aims, or accustomed patterns of piety \citep{williams2002}.}

\subpar{(2) \textbf{Epistemic humility (\textit{Humilitas Scientiae}):} The conscious acknowledgment of one's own perspectivity and fallibility. The insight that one's own understanding is always only ``the best we presently know'' guards against a dogmatic claim to absoluteness, and keeps one open to better reasons \citep{popper1963}.}

\subpar{(3) \textbf{Hermeneutical magnanimity (\textit{Nobilitas Mentis}):} A generous, unprejudiced disposition (cf. εὐγενέστεροι in Acts 17:11). One meets the text and its witnesses neither with cynical condescension nor with blind, slavish obedience, but with sincere respect, on equal footing \citep{davidson1974,jacobs2001}.}

\subpar{(4) \textbf{Tolerance of ambiguity (\textit{Tolerantia Ambiguitatis}):} The ability to endure open questions, gaps in knowledge, and unresolved tensions left in the text, without prematurely and forcibly dissolving them through artificial harmonizations or pragmatic shortcuts \citep{frenkelbrunswik1949}.}

\apthsec{24}{Historical Heuristic: The Credal Compatibility of the Findings}{heuristik-befund} Research faithful to its object leads, with high probability, to findings that coincide with the creeds of the ancient church (the symbola). On the basis of the inference to the best explanation, we frame the well-founded expectation -- a heuristic -- that a historically honest study of the biblical illocutions, in their central claims, does not lead to a dismantling of faith in revelation. It is to be expected, with high probability, that the open-ended historical reconstruction will yield findings mirrored in the essential content of the ancient church's creeds, the Apostles' Creed and the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed. This agreement is not forced dogmatically; it arises as the rationally expectable consequence of historically honest work on the text. The heuristic is expressly no more than a prospect, and must not be mistaken for a circularity that anticipates the result of the method: the method itself remains open-ended at every moment, and this expectation proves nothing. It is, rather, an honest reckoning with whether a method framed so open-endedly might not -- against the faith of those who carry it out -- lead after all into heresy or apostasy. The author, in any case, has not fallen away from the faith to this day, and has never approached the texts with apologetic intent. From the honestly understood findings and from existential faith (cf. §~\ref{sec:kategorial}) there finally grow the applied disciplines, such as ethics and ecclesiology.

\apthsec{25}{Expectation concerning Infallibility}{infallibilitaet} Like the finding in §~\ref{sec:heuristik-befund}, this statement, too, runs as a well-founded expectation and not as a dogma. The \apth speaks here deliberately of the technical term \textit{infallibility}, and renders it pragmatically as \textit{unerring reliability in the central questions of faith and life}; the widespread rendering of the term as an absolute freedom from error in every detail of wording is misleading. The expectation rests on an induction: owing to the historical development and selection of the canon, texts that grossly contradicted the central questions of faith were, in the course of the early church's process of elimination, precisely not admitted to the canon. From this one may conclude, inductively, that an original, minimal form of infallibility -- the reliability of the canon in its core statements -- can in all likelihood be adopted by proponents of the \apth, so as to take up a clear position within the theological landscape. It remains, however, expressly no dogma, and it depends heavily on the definition chosen: whether one takes infallibility maximally (not a single error in any place where the Bible touches on the attainment of salvation and gods basic characteristics) or minimally (no error in what is decisive) alters the claim at its root. That restraint is in order is shown above all by the findings of historical-critical research: wherever its representatives survey the ethical and other questions of the canonical writings, internal tensions and contradictions come quickly to light \citep{dunn1977,kaesemann1951,ehrman2009}. Proponents of the \apth would -- methodologically consistently -- in all likelihood find such findings too, and would, in accordance with §~\ref{sec:tiefen-harmonisierung}, let them stand without harmonizing them. Precisely for this reason, infallibility remains for the \apth an expectation and not an axiom.

\section*{Conclusion}
\begin{quote}
This manifesto is the fruit of a deep search for intellectual coherence and inner certainty. For those trained in the categories of modern logic, epistemology, and the philosophy of science, the desire for a load-bearing foundation of faith becomes an existential task. The search for genuine historical and logical integrity proves thereby to be the solid ground on which a faith answerable to reason may stand in full clarity.  

Precisely for this reason the approach bears the name \textit{Approbatio Testimonii Historica} -- the recognition of the historical testimony. The term captures the intent: it joins the incorruptible examination and confirmation (\textit{approbatio}) with deep respect for the concrete statement of the ancient eyewitnesses (\textit{testimonium}), who left behind their experience in space and time. It is the recognition that God's action leaves traces in real history, and that the tools of reason serve to understand these testimonies in their ancient linguistic intent.  The \textit{Approbatio Testimonii Historica} takes the instruments of historical scholarship as a gift of creation. It employs reason to lay bare the historical explanatory power of the biblical testimonies, and to lead the believer into a sincere, fearless encounter with the sources.

Knowledge remains an ongoing process, and by no means are all questions answered, or all tensions resolved. This approach understands itself not as a militant confession, nor as an infallible dogma that passes judgment on others, but as a peaceful, peace-making space of invitation to the shared search for truth. Where scientific rigor, openness, and the certainty of faith go hand in hand, the truth unfolds its full radiance. Therefore:
\end{quote}

{\large\itshape Fides non timet veritatem.}\\[0.3em]
{\small (Faith does not fear the truth)}
\end{center}
